# Inspiration library — 5 balanced families

Inspiration only. Never trace, reconstruct, or recolor any source. Extract useful construction principles, then remix into original topology. Family recipes and their counts are illustrative, not mandatory layouts or a closed vocabulary. Select only ideas fitting the approved brief; validate the resulting mark at its actual target sizes.

## Anti-bias rule (important)

ASCII / pixel is one possible family, not the default. Choose a brief-proportionate number of distinct directions and relevant families as described in the entrypoint; do not require a minimum family count or a fixed concept quota. Explain the construction principle and how the topology differs from its inspiration. A precise approved brief may justify a single family or immediate production.

## When to read

Read AFTER intake context is refined (meaning, audience, usage sizes known), DURING the interview, BEFORE the concept phase. Read only entries relevant to unresolved visual choices.

## How to use

1. Re-read the **When to use** of each family and shortlist families that resonate with the user's own words from the interview (nuance matters — never force a keyword match; if the user hesitates between two directions, keep both families in the batch).
2. Translate the matched families' rules into IconDraft recipes (grid, void, cut, anomaly), not copied shapes.
3. Develop genuinely different directions only where comparison is useful; vary topology, voids and balance rather than just recoloring. Name relevant inspiration principles without imposing a count.
4. Judge first in monochrome at the approved smallest size and representative larger uses; add depth or gradients only when topology remains readable.

Each family below follows the same template: transferable rules, **When to use**, **How to use**.

## Family 1 — 3D geometric logos

Local board: `assets/inspiration/3d-geometric-board.png` (24 tiles: stairs, pinwheels, torus, sparkle, faceted diamond, pyramid, eye, stacked domes, folded hexagon, X-petals, spiky discs, ribbon-S, lips, sprout-in-bowl…).

Transferable rules (steal the rule, not the tile):
- One primitive + one material cue: torus / pyramid / half-dome / folded plane rendered by a single highlight or fold line, not by full shading.
- Soft-inflatables vs hard-faceted contrast: pick one per direction, never mix both in one mark.
- Rotational modules with one break: 3–4 petals/folds around a void, one petal shifted, cut, or recolored (controlled anomaly).
- Fold lines as structure: internal seams that divide a solid into 2–4 faces (diamond split, X-crease cushion, hexagon fan).
- Void as active shape: ring hole, eye pupil, square gap between arcs, gap between stacked domes.
- Single floating satellite: small detached element (pill above dome, dot in torus) for tension; only one per mark.

**When to use:** the user talks about depth, tactility, playfulness, or a product that lives on screens where subtle volume reads well (app icons, avatars, hero marks); brand wants friendly-modern rather than corporate-strict.

**How to use:** build the flat silhouette + void first in IconDraft; add exactly one depth cue (one fold, one highlight, one shadow). If it fails flat, it fails. Never render full 3D shading in the SVG.

Web echoes: glass/plastic/inflatable volume — https://amadine.com/useful-articles/logo-design-trends · distorted / elongated cubes — https://www.andacademy.com/resources/blog/graphic-design/14-logo-design-trends/ · atmospheric gradients — https://digitalsynopsis.com/design/logo-design-trends-2026/

## Family 2 — Fluid geometric minimalism (movement + structure)

Local board: `assets/inspiration/fluid-geometric-minimalism.png` — 4 marks: `bollo` (pillow store), `nik` (juice), `edwoley` (shampoo), `xenaz` (3-blade pinwheel).

Transferable rules:
- `bollo`: one thick continuous ribbon folding into two teardrop voids of different sizes; varying stroke width creates movement, closed outer contour keeps structure. Steal: one-ribbon-two-voids.
- `nik`: rigid frame, fluid cuts — blocky outer caps with diagonal liquid incisions. Steal: hard-outside / soft-inside tension.
- `edwoley`: shards held by a shared invisible triangle; negative-space zigzag as the real subject. Steal: fragments aligned by an unseen container.
- `xenaz`: three identical comma-blades rotated 120°, meeting at an off-center point. Steal: rotational repetition with teardrop terminals.
- Shared: single weight, single color, one curve logic per mark; generous rounding mixed with one sharp cut.

**When to use:** the user wants softness with authority — lifestyle, food, wellness, boutique brands — or describes the mark as "flowing", "organic", "alive" while still needing a clean silhouette at small sizes.

**How to use:** one closed path or 2–3 rotated copies of one module; teardrop terminals (round + pointed ends); sketch void-first, then wrap the ribbon around it. Keep one curve logic per mark.

## Family 3 — Machine-inspired / algorithmic

Local ref: `assets/inspiration/algorithmic-dither-bird.png` — gull silhouette built from modulated horizontal scanlines: dense black mass where dark, thinning broken lines where light, two solid eye/block accents.

Transferable rules:
- Form from density, not outline: vary line weight, dash length, or spacing over a field; silhouette emerges.
- One generative rule per mark: scanlines, concentric disturbance, dot-dither gradient, hatch field bent by a curve.
- Threshold accents: 1–2 solid black islands inside the field as anchors (eye, core).
- Edge breakup: let contour dissolve into the field on one side only; keep the opposite side clean for legibility.
- Micro-size fallback mandatory: every algorithmic direction ships with a solid-silhouette variant for 16–32 px.

**When to use:** the user talks about generative systems, data, craft-meets-code, or wants a mark that feels "computed" rather than hand-drawn; tech or research contexts where a rule-based story sells the identity.

**How to use:** grouped parallel strokes with varying `stroke-width` / `stroke-dasharray`, clipped to the silhouette; describe the rule in the recipe (never hand-place dozens of lines). Always present the solid fallback alongside.

## Family 4 — Pixel-sharp / ASCII / iso / brutalist (technical vocabulary, NOT a default)

Use as a *style* only when it fits the brief, without a fixed per-batch quota. Its everyday value is method (grid discipline, IconDraft rasters), not looks.

Reference texts:
- **Pixel sharp precision:** grid-based, ultra-crisp logos reflect digital fluency and technical clarity. Appeals to SaaS, tech startups, and data-driven brands, offering structure in visual noise.
- Iso 2.5D construction: 30° diagonals, 120° axes, parallels stay parallel — https://flat-icons.com/what-are-isometric-icons/
- ASCII revival, type-as-texture (density/rhythm logic for rasters, not final art) — https://onepagelove.com/tag/ascii
- Blocky pixel alphabets (block unit + southeast offset-echo) — https://dribbble.com/search/pixel-font · modular alphabets — https://stock.adobe.com/search?k=modular+typeface
- Brutalist offset-echo masses — https://www.behance.net/gallery/188879055/Retro-Futurism-Brutalist-Collection · neobrutalism vectors — https://www.vecteezy.com/free-vector/neo-brutalism

**When to use:** the user describes precision, systems, performance, dashboards, developer tools — or explicitly asks for retro-tech / ASCII / pixel. Pixel-sharp fits SaaS and data brands; brutalist offset-echo fits bold youth or event identities; ASCII-forward only on explicit request.

**How to use:** snap everything to one strict grid unit; keep spacing deliberate and letterforms clean; introduce warmth elsewhere (color, motion) to avoid coldness. For ASCII: use character density for the *draft raster* comparison, not as final SVG geometry — unless the brief is ASCII-art itself, in which case design the character set first (3–4 glyphs max) and a solid fallback.

## Family 5 — Morphing / adaptive systems

Reference text: dynamic marks that shift shape or composition across contexts reflect how brands now live in motion. Rather than one static logo, designers create systems.

Local board: `assets/inspiration/adaptive-morphing-board.png` — fluid black ribbon monogram (one form, variable stress) + WORK/WORK/SHOP poster (same wordmark sliced, offset, and mirrored across rows; bang bang education / identity workshop).

Transferable rules:
- One core form, parameterized variations: define the invariant (skeleton, void, proportions), then vary slice offset, weight stress, or duplication per context.
- Slice-and-shift: duplicate the mark in 2–3 horizontal bands with small lateral offsets; keep at least one band intact so the wordmark stays readable.
- Stress morphing: same ribbon skeleton rendered fat/thin/italic by moving two control handles; terminals stay consistent.
- Duplication rhythm: repeat–mirror–truncate (WORK / WORK / SHOP) to suggest a system, not a glitch — alignment grid stays visible.
- Static-first: every variant must work as a frozen SVG; motion is a bonus, never the carrier of meaning.

**When to use:** the user talks about a brand that lives in motion, campaigns, seasons, multi-platform systems — or wants "one logo, many moods" rather than a single frozen mark. Digital-first and event identities especially.

**How to use:** deliver only approved core/variant outputs, with any changed parameter explained (offset, slice count, stress axis). Compare the requested variants at their actual smallest uses; do not generate extra variants to satisfy a quota.

## Anti-copy checklist

- No traced outlines, no identical topology, no recolor-only variants.
- Change element count, void shape, spatial logic, and balance — never only color or corner radius.
- If a direction resembles a board tile or linked logo, discard and re-derive from the rule alone.
